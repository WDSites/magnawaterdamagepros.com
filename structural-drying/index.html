The science of drying a structure

What Structural Drying Is

Structural drying is the work of bringing wet building materials back to a normal moisture level after the water is gone. It covers framing, subfloors, drywall, plaster, concrete and cabinets. The goal is not a room that feels dry but materials that read dry on a meter.

It differs from water mitigation, which is the emergency response that stops the spread. Drying is the controlled, measured phase that follows, and it can take several days.

How Drying Works

Water leaves a building by evaporation. Air movers push dry air across wet surfaces, dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the air and hold it in a tank or drain it away, and the process repeats until the materials equalize with a dry reference. If the room is cold or the air is already saturated, drying stalls.

Safety Steps While Drying Is Underway

  • Keep cords and equipment out of puddles and stay away from damp outlets.
  • Do not move air movers, because their placement is planned.
  • Keep doors and windows closed in the drying zone unless the crew says otherwise.
  • Do not turn off equipment to save noise.
  • Watch for musty odors, which suggest materials are still wet.
  • Call the dispatch line if a machine stops or a reading looks wrong.

Dry Air And Old Homes In Magna

Salt Lake City gets only about 15.5 inches of precipitation a year in NOAA records, and winter indoor air is often dry. That helps surfaces dry but does not reach inside a wall cavity or a concrete slab, where moisture lingers.

Older Magna houses often combine plaster, dated insulation and partial basements, which dry at different rates. The crew chooses equipment placement by material, not by room size alone.

Why Wood And Concrete Dry At Different Speeds

Materials hold water in different ways. Drywall is thin and gives up moisture quickly, hardwood and framing lumber take longer, and a concrete slab can keep releasing moisture for days after the surface looks dry. A good plan sets a target reading for each material instead of one answer for the whole room.

This is why a company that checks only a single wall can leave a wet subfloor behind. Ask where readings were taken, and look for the same spots to be measured on each visit.

What Changes The Cost

  • The size of the area and the number of wet rooms.
  • Materials, since concrete and hardwood dry slowly.
  • Equipment count and days on site.
  • How quickly drying started after the water stopped.
  • Whether the wall cavities need to be opened.

Insurance And Documentation

Daily readings and photos give an adjuster a record of what was wet and when it dried. A policy that excludes flood will not pay for drying after groundwater, so the cause of the water matters before any claim. Keep the drying log with your policy papers.

Mistakes To Avoid

  • Stopping equipment because the surface feels dry.
  • Using a household fan in place of a dehumidifier.
  • Closing wet drywall back up before it has dried.
  • Skipping the final reading.

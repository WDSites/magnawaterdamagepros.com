Salt Lake City Homes And The Water Damage They Get

Salt Lake City mixes housing from different centuries, and that matters for water damage. Many valley homes date from before World War II, and the Avenues historic district between 6th Avenue and South Temple holds a lot of Victorian-era houses. Original plumbing in houses that old may be galvanized steel or cast iron, and sewer laterals can be clay.

The west side, including Rose Park, Poplar Grove and Glendale, is more working-class housing built across the middle of the last century. Basements there often take on water from sewer backups and window wells, while the eastern benches see water from roofs and freeze-related pipe failures.

Climate, Creeks And Flood History

The city's long-term average is about 15.5 inches of precipitation a year, with roughly 127 days at or below freezing. Pipes that split in a cold snap often leak only after the thaw.

In 1983, City Creek burst its banks and city engineers turned several downtown streets into channels for the water. The county's flood control office still manages the creeks, canals and rivers, while the city handles flooded streets. Check msc.fema.gov for your address, since risk differs from block to block.

The lowest part of the city, near the Jordan River and the Great Salt Lake, sits at about 4,210 feet, while the average elevation is closer to 4,327. That drop means water from higher streets often pools in west-side basements.

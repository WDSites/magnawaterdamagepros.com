What We Handle In Commercial Spaces

A commercial water loss is a water loss with a clock on it, because a closed shop or office loses income. We handle extraction, drying and cleanup in small commercial spaces such as offices, storefronts, shops and small warehouses. Larger or specialized facilities may need a company built for that scale, and we say so on the phone.

We do not promise reopening dates, and we do not claim experience with any industry or client. We describe the work that can be done and let the readings set the schedule.

How Commercial Losses Start

Supply line and fixture failures

Restroom, break room and sprinkler-adjacent lines can fail and flood a floor plate.

Roof and ceiling leaks

A leak above a drop ceiling can soak tiles, insulation and equipment.

Sewer and drain backups

Floor drains in shops and storefronts can back up and contaminate a space.

Water heater and HVAC leaks

Mechanical rooms and rooftop units can drip into the space below.

Safety Steps For The First Hour

  • Close the main water valve for the space or the building and confirm no one is in the wet area.
  • Switch off power to wet circuits only if you can do it safely, or ask the utility to disconnect it.
  • Move computers, files and inventory off the floor and above the water line.
  • Photograph the damage and keep a list of affected property for your insurer.
  • Contact the building owner or property manager if the space is leased.
  • Call the dispatch line at (385) 988-9410 for extraction and drying.

How The Work Is Done

Commercial work follows the same method as residential, adjusted to the floors and finishes involved. Extraction removes standing water, meters map wet materials and drying equipment runs on carpet tile, drywall and subfloor. Hard floors are cleaned and sanitized, and wet insulation or ceiling tiles come out.

Step 01

Assess

Meters and a walkthrough set the scope and the order of rooms.

Step 02

Extract

Pumps and vacuums remove standing water from floors.

Step 03

Remove wet material

Soaked ceiling tiles, carpet tile and insulation are taken out.

Step 04

Dry and sign off

Equipment runs with daily readings until the materials match a dry reference.

For commercial drying, ANSI/IICRC S500 serves as a reference, and a crew should explain what is removed and what can stay.

Main Street And Magna Buildings

Magna became an incorporated city on May 1, 2024, and its Main Street is lined with older brick commercial blocks. The Empress Theatre is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, which the National Park Service records as 1985. Roofs and plumbing in buildings of that age have been patched over decades, so a leak may travel a long way before it shows.

Commercial shutoffs inside a building belong to the owner, not the utility, so know where yours is before you need it.

Protecting Records And Stock

Paper, boxed inventory and electronics wick water from the floor faster than most owners expect. Lift them onto shelving or tables, and keep original documents separate so they can be dried properly. Photograph everything before it moves, since your insurer will ask what was affected.

If the space is shared with other tenants, tell the property manager right away. Water often crosses walls and ceilings, and one report can start the work for the whole building.

What Changes The Cost

  • Floor area and the number of rooms affected.
  • Flooring type, such as carpet tile, concrete or vinyl.
  • Water category and any sanitizing.
  • Drying time and equipment.
  • Rebuild scope handled afterward by a contractor.

Insurance And Leases

Commercial property policies differ from homeowners policies, and a lease may say who handles building damage. Ask your insurer and your landlord who is responsible before work starts. Keep photos, moisture readings and an inventory of what was damaged.

Mistakes To Avoid

  • Leaving wet ceiling tiles in place to save money.
  • Moving electronics back before the area is dry.
  • Assuming the landlord has already called a company.
  • Skipping photos of stock and equipment.
